El Niño in Latin America: Same
Ocean, Three Different Guys

 El Niño is a recurring anomaly in Pacific Ocean temperatures that generates highly
heterogeneous climatic effects across regions, particularly in Latin America. These
differences are compounded by varying levels of exposure and vulnerability across and within
economies. As a result, El Niño represents a significant and persistent economic shock
whose impacts depend critically on local conditions and economic structures.

Recent observations and forecasts suggest that the tropical Pacific may once again be
transitioning towards El Niño conditions, as sea surface temperatures (SST) in the El
Niño 3.4 region continue to move above the 0.5ºC threshold above the historical mean
(Figure 1). Based on a scenario analysis performed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration (NOAA), uncertainty remains regarding the intensity and persistence of the
event1. While attributing changes in El Niño dynamics directly to anthropogenic climate change
is an active area of scientific research, several studies suggest that since the mid-twentieth
century the frequency and intensity of extreme El Niño events may have increased. However,
history shows that even moderate El Niño episodes can have meaningful economic
consequences. For policymakers, firms and financial institutions, the relevant question
is not whether Pacific waters are warming, but how this common climatic signal
translates into local economic outcomes.

While El Niño originates as a singular oceanic event in the Pacific, its physical
manifestation across Latin America is fundamentally heterogeneous.2 To isolate these
varying climate dynamics, we conducted an empirical assessment of precipitation and
temperature anomalies across BBVA footprint countries in the area (see Box 1). The results
confirm that the physical transmission channels differ substantially: severe droughts in
Colombia, excess rainfall in Argentina, and intense warming in Peru.

The broader literature increasingly suggests that El Niño should be viewed not only as a
weather event but also as a macroeconomic shock. Early work by Cashin, Mohaddes and
Raissi (2015)3 showed that El Niño shocks generate heterogeneous responses in GDP and
inflation across countries.

More recent evidence by Callahan and Mankin (2023)4 highlight important nonlinearities,
showing that economic responses intensify disproportionately during strong El Niño episodes
and that the resulting losses are often larger and more persistent than the gains associated
with La Niña events.

Those features are particularly relevant for Latin America, where, apart from
heterogeneous climate effects, the economic exposure patterns vary significantly
across economies.

Argentina represents perhaps the most nuanced case in the region. Unlike many tropical
economies, El Niño is often associated with above-average rainfall across the Pampas,
reducing drought risk during the growing season. As a result, agricultural yields are frequently
more positive.

The empirical evidence is remarkably consistent. Podestá et al. (1999) documented a
strong relationship between El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) phases, precipitation
anomalies and yields of maize, sorghum and soybean in central-eastern Argentina. More
recently, Cornejo (2023) estimated that each additional degree of Pacific warming can increase
average national soybean yields by roughly 7.7%, although effects vary substantially across
regions. Likewise, Francia Laurenzo et al. (2025) estimate that El Niño conditions can increase
maize production in the Pampas by approximately 6 million tons relative to neutral years.

Consistent with these findings, Figure 2 shows that El Niño episodes broadly coincide with
periods of stronger growth in primary export volumes.

The above described agricultural channel is sufficiently important to appear even in
international macroeconomic studies. Cashin et al. (2015) find that Argentina exhibits a
positive increase in real GDP growth (+0.31 p.p) one year after an El Niño shock, making one
of the few positive responses among major economies.

Nevertheless, El Niño should not be interpreted as an unambiguously positive shock.
The same rainfall that benefits crop yields also increases flood risk. According to the World
Bank, Argentina loses approximately US$1 billion annually due to flooding, affecting
agriculture, infrastructure and trade. Consequently, El Niño tends to shift the country’s climate
risk profile from drought-related losses toward excess-water losses.

Colombia’s exposure is fundamentally different. El Niño episodes are typically associated
with lower rainfall and higher temperatures, particularly in the Andean and Caribbean regions.
The resulting drought conditions affect agriculture directly but also propagate through
Colombia’s hydropower-dependent electricity system.

The magnitude of the economic impact on aggregate GDP remains subject to debate.
Some official estimates, such as those from Departamento Nacional de Planeación (DNP)5,
have quantified non-negligible output losses during the 2015 El Niño episode6. However, these
estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they rely on input-output matrices which may
not fully capture the dynamic effects on GDP. In fact, BBVA Research estimates do not find
that El Niño episodes have a statistically significant aggregate impact on GDP7.

Perhaps the most important macroeconomic transmission channel is inflation. Using a
nonlinear framework, Abril-Salcedo, Melo-Velandia and Parra-Amado (2019) find that strong El
Niño events generate significant food price pressures with lags of six to nine months,
producing an accumulated impact of roughly 465 basis points on food inflation. This
mechanism helps explain why El Niño has repeatedly appeared in Banco de La República’s
(Colombia’s Central Bank) discussions about inflationary supply shocks. Consistent with these
findings, Figure 3 shows that episodes of El Niño tend to be followed by increases in food
inflation, which often exceed movements in headline inflation.

The energy sector amplifies these effects. Recent work by Sánchez et al. (2025) documents
statistically significant ENSO effects in inflows to Colombia’s 15 largest hydropower reservoirs.
Lower water availability raises generation costs and increases the likelihood of electricity
market stress, reinforcing the connection between climate variability, inflation and economic
activity losses.

Peru provides the clearest example of El Niño as a systematic macroeconomic shock.
Unlike Argentina and Colombia, Peru is directly exposed to the oceanic component of ENSO.
Warmer coastal waters simultaneously affect fisheries, rainfall patterns, agriculture,
infrastructure and inflation.

Historical losses have been severe, although their magnitude depends critically on the
geographical expression and timing of the event. According to Peru’s Ministry of
Environment (MINAM in Spanish)  the 1982-83 El Niño generated damages equivalent to
11.6% of GDP, while the 1997-98 event caused losses equivalent to 6.2% of GDP. In both
cases, warming in the central Pacific coincided with warm waters off Peru (Niño 1+2 region)
during the rainy season, triggering torrential rainfall, widespread infrastructure damage and
major economic disruptions.

However, not all El Niño episodes generate comparable macroeconomic effects in Peru.
Episodes characterized by warming in the Niño 3.4 region but limited warming along Peruvian
coast have historically produced much more moderate impacts, often concentrated in drought
conditions affecting parts of the southern Andes. Conversely, coastal warming in the Niño 1+2
region can generate substantial economic losses even when anomalies in the central Pacific
are relatively limited, as illustrated by the 2017 Coastal El Niño.

The fisheries channel is particularly important. ENFEN (Comisión Multisectorial encargada
del Estudio Nacional del Fenómeno “El Niño”) documents that the 2017 Coastal El Niño
reduced coastal upwelling, lowered marine productivity and displaced anchovy stocks

Recent research by the Banco Central de Reserva del Perú (BCRP, Peru’s central bank)
shows that these sectoral disruptions aggregate into a broader macroeconomic supply
shock. Large El Niño Costero episodes simultaneously reduce output and increase inflation,
creating the classic policy trade-off faced by central banks. The effects are highly
heterogeneous across sectors: fisheries and agriculture experience the most immediate
contractions, while negative spillovers to non-primary sectors emerge with a lag and can
persist for several quarters. The evidence also suggests that severe El Niño events affect
inflation expectations, raising the risk that temporary food and supply shocks become
embedded in broader price-setting behavior. As a result, El Niño in Peru should be viewed not
simply as a climate event but as a recurrent source of macroeconomic volatility that influences
growth, inflation and monetary policy dynamics alike.

In conclusion, El Niño should be understood not as a uniform climatic disturbance but
as a highly heterogeneous climate phenomenon. It generates very different weather
outcomes across Latin America, including droughts, floods or temperature anomalies.
Consequently, the economic impacts are equally diverse. Countries are exposed not to
the same shock, but to different climatic expressions of the same phenomenon, which
helps explain why El Niño affects agricultural production, inflation, growth, and financial
conditions differently across Argentina, Colombia, and Peru. Recognizing both the
climatic and economic heterogeneity of El Niño is essential for designing effective
macroeconomic policies, climate-adaptation strategies, and financial risk-management
frameworks.

Box 1. An empirical assessment of El Niño
climate dynamics

To better understand the distinct physical drivers behind the divergent economic outcomes
observed in Argentina, Colombia, and Peru, we conduct an empirical analysis of the
response of climate variables to El Niño episodes in each country.

We estimate three country-specific Structural Vector Autoregression (SVAR) models for
the period spanning from 1951 to 2024. Each model is identified with a recursive
identification strategy (Cholesky decomposition). The system incorporates three
variables at monthly frequency, ordered as follows to reflect the transmission
mechanism:

1.  Sea Surface Temperature (SST) anomaly in the Niño 3.4 region (common to the

three models)

2.  Mean temperature (country-specific)

3.  Mean precipitation (country-specific)

SST anomalies enter the model in levels, while temperature and precipitation are
expressed in year-on-year growth rates.

The optimal lag order is determined using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the
Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), selecting the maximum lag length suggested by
either test. Consequently, the models incorporate 134 lags for Colombia, and 85 lags for
both Peru and Argentina.

Figure B1 reports the responses of sea surface temperature (SST), mean temperature,
and precipitation to a 0.5°C SST anomaly in Colombia, Peru, and Argentina. The SST
response is, as expected, similar across countries, peaking at around 0.6-0.7°C and
gradually dissipating after 12–15 months (consistent with the fact that El Niño tends to last
around 12 months).

However, the local climate effects differ substantially. In Colombia, the shock
increases temperatures and generates a pronounced decline in precipitation, consistent
with the drought conditions typically associated with El Niño events. In contrast, Argentina
experiences a strong increase in precipitation, in line with the favorable agricultural
conditions often observed during El Niño years.

Peru shows a significant increase in temperature but an insignificant effect in the estimated
precipitation response. This partly reflects the fact that the model is identified using Niño
3.4 conditions, while Peru’s most severe rainfall events are more closely linked to warming
